Section 01

Context and Problem Statement

ATS Group is a French industrial conveyor systems integrator. In early 2026, the company responded to a client tender in Pune, India, for the delivery of a complete intralogistics sorting system — capable of processing approximately 480,000 articles per day across a single production shift.

The client's facility already includes an ASRS (Automated Storage and Retrieval System) — a mechanized high-density storage installation where crates containing articles are stored in racks and retrieved by automated cranes. The ASRS is controlled by its own industrial PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) and connects to the rest of the warehouse via a conveyor network. Beyond this, nothing exists. No sorting software, no operator stations, no decision logic. The tender specifies the output — articles correctly sorted into outbound orders, trucks departing on schedule — and leaves the system architecture open.

My role at ATS covers the full software and PTL hardware scope of the project: designing the WMS from scratch, specifying and assembling the PTL stations, and building the integration layer that connects the software to the physical equipment.

What a Put-to-Light system is

A PTL (Put-to-Light) station is an operator-guided sorting workstation. Each station consists of a rack of bins — in this project, 10 bins per station — each equipped with an LED display module and a confirmation button. When a crate arrives from the conveyor, the WMS identifies which orders require articles from that crate and illuminates the corresponding bin modules, indicating the destination bin and the quantity to place. The operator places the articles and presses the confirmation button; the light extinguishes and the WMS records the completion. The system runs 24 such stations in parallel.

The hardware is straightforward. The difficulty lies elsewhere: at any given moment, the WMS must decide which crate to release onto the conveyor, to which station, and in what order — while simultaneously managing hundreds of active orders across 24 stations, respecting ASRS retrieval constraints, and ensuring all orders in a given wave are completed before the truck departure deadline.

The core engineering problem

The system involves three distinct technical layers that must operate as a single coherent whole. SAP, the client's ERP, sends orders to the WMS as transactional database records — a standard IT operation with no real-time constraints. The ASRS PLC and PTL controllers, on the other hand, are hard real-time systems: a command sent too late or out of sequence has immediate physical consequences that cannot be undone. The WMS sits between these two worlds and must satisfy both simultaneously.

This is the IT/OT integration problem — the structural tension between transactional information systems (IT) and real-time operational hardware (OT). No validated architectural pattern exists in the academic literature for a fully custom-built system of this type. The architecture had to be reasoned from first principles.

The starting point was a client tender, not a defined specification. Every architectural decision documented in this portfolio was made from scratch, with no prior codebase and no reference implementation to build on.
